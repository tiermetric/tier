The canonical TIER rubric (v1 and opt-in v2)

This document is the versioned, normative rubric for TIER's outcome weights and work-type taxonomy. It is the weight-side analogue of the reference price table: the price table pins the dollars, this rubric pins the weighted points, and a TIER number is only comparable across two measurements when both stamps match.

  • Rubric version: disabled Event 3 policy uses 1 (the legacy scoring.RubricVersion constant); enabled, fully resolved stored policy uses 2.
  • Where it is stamped: every GET /api/v1/scores response carries a top-level rubric: {version} block next to price_table: {version, effective_date}.
  • When it must change: any edit to the weight scale or the work-type taxonomy below must bump scoring.RubricVersion. That is the point of the stamp, not a side effect — a silent edit would make yesterday's scores falsely comparable to today's.

There is no absolute "good" TIER. This rubric deliberately defines no reference cohort and no good/ok/poor band. A weighted point is org-local by construction; a bare TIER of 40 is neither good nor bad on its own. What is defensible is a self-relative comparison — the same org over time, subject to matched measurement definitions — expressed through cost_per_point. See What you may and may not compare.

Why the rubric is versioned (the exploit it closes)

With Event 3 scoring disabled, TIER's numerator is Σ(weight × quality). Enabled scoring uses the allocated points in rubric 2. The weight comes from a size label (or a heuristic fallback — see conventions.md). If the label→weight mapping is merely a "convention", two orgs can apply it with different generosity: a generous-labeling org calls the same change size/l (5) that a strict org calls size/m (3). At identical real efficiency, the generous org posts a higher TIER. Without a stamped rubric, a cross-org comparison silently rewards labeling culture, not engineering yield — and TIER drifts into people-ranking, the one thing it must not do.

Two distinct mechanisms address this — do not conflate them:

  • Normativity (this document's worked examples) provides common definitions. It does not establish that two organisations calibrate labels equally, and TIER neither validates nor enforces inter-rater equivalence. Two organisations do not compare, even when their version stamps match.
  • The version stamp (rubric.version on the wire) is provenance: it makes a change to the rubric definition detectable over time, so a self-over-time or matched-version comparison can confirm the weighting did not drift. It does not, by itself, detect a cross-org generosity difference — two orgs on the same binary stamp the same version however each labels — nor a local size_labels remap (see the caveat under Size → weight).

A rubric.version mismatch identifies a definition change. Matching versions do not establish shared calibration or comparable capture and task mix; a cross-organisation cost_per_point reading remains unsupported.

Size → weight

The weight of a merged PR is its size, read from a GitHub label (case-insensitive, size/ prefix optional). The scale is fixed — these five values and no others:

Label Weight What it means (canonical calibration)
size/xs / xs 0.5 Trivial: a one-line fix, a typo, a config flag, a dependency bump.
size/s / s 1 Small: a localized change to one function/file; a focused bug fix with a test.
size/m / m 3 Medium: a self-contained feature or fix spanning a few files; the everyday unit of work.
size/l / l 5 Large: a feature touching several components, or a non-trivial refactor with migration.
size/xl / xl 8 Extra-large: a subsystem, a cross-cutting change, or a multi-day effort landed as one PR.

With no recognized label, TIER falls back to the lines/files heuristic (see conventions.md); explicit labels are preferred because they let a reviewer correct the heuristic (a two-line change to a critical path is not a two-line typo fix).

What rubric.version does and does not assert. The stamp names the canonical rubric compiled into the binary — this scale and taxonomy. It is not a fingerprint of an operator's local configuration. The weights themselves are locked to the {0.5, 1, 3, 5, 8} scale (#244, enforced at startup), but an operator MAY rename the labels that map onto them via outcomes.size_labels (e.g. map a house label epic to 8). Renaming which label triggers a weight does not change the meaning of a weight, so the stamp stays honest for that case. A local convention that maps a size to a different weight than this document's calibration (e.g. treating everyday work as l/5 rather than m/3) is a divergence the operator owns: the stamp cannot detect it, so such an org must not read its cost_per_point against another party's. A config-fingerprint stamp is a possible future refinement; cross-organisation comparison remains unsupported even with matched versions and reconciled labels.

Worked examples (per size)

Assume a clean outcome (quality = 1.0; see Quality multiplier).

  1. A size/s (weight 1) bug fix that cost $2.00 of AI compute.
    • weighted_points = 1 × 1.0 = 1
    • cost_per_point = 2.00 / 1 = $2.00 per point
    • tier = 1 / (2.00/1000) = 500
  2. A size/l (weight 5) feature that cost $12.00.
    • weighted_points = 5 × 1.0 = 5
    • cost_per_point = 12.00 / 5 = $2.40 per point
    • tier = 5 / (12.00/1000) ≈ 417
  3. A size/xs (weight 0.5) typo fix that cost $0.40.
    • weighted_points = 0.5
    • cost_per_point = 0.40 / 0.5 = $0.80 per point
    • tier = 0.5 / (0.40/1000) = 1250

Note example 3's TIER of 1250 — a tiny outcome against tiny cost produces an enormous ratio. This is why one raw TIER value can't be read on its own, and why the evidence floor (#133: at least 3 outcomes and at least $5 of cost) exists. cost_per_point is the steadier unit: across the three examples it stays in the $0.80–$2.40 band even as TIER swings from 417 to 1250.

Work-type taxonomy

Outcomes are partitioned by work_type so a category is only ever compared to the same category (a security outcome against other security work, never against feature work). The canonical taxonomy is these seven categories, in order:

work_type Scope
feature New user-facing or internal capability. The default when unlabeled.
bug Correcting incorrect behavior in shipped code.
security Vulnerability remediation, hardening, auth/crypto work.
incident Production incident response and remediation.
tech-debt Refactoring, cleanup, dependency and platform maintenance.
research Spikes, investigations, prototypes — exploratory work.
compliance Regulatory, audit, privacy, and policy-driven work.

The same size→weight scale applies within every work type; the taxonomy segments what is compared to what, it does not change how a point is earned.

Worked example (per work_type)

A developer merges, in one window: a size/m feature ($9 cost), a size/s bug ($1.50), and a size/l security fix ($20).

Segment weight cost cost_per_point
feature 3 $9.00 $3.00 / point
bug 1 $1.50 $1.50 / point
security 5 $20.00 $4.00 / point

Each cost_per_point is meaningful only against the same segment in another window. A pooled cost_per_point across the three is not a supported comparison — the /scores work_types array is the authoritative surface, and the pooled top-level rows are a back-compat summary, not a cross-type ranking (#187).

Quality multiplier

Weight is scaled by a quality multiplier derived from quality events (#134), so weighted_points = Σ(weight × quality):

Quality floor Multiplier
Clean 1.0
CI failure 0.7
Strategic revert 0.8
Quality revert 0.1

The quality floors are part of the rubric: changing them also bumps rubric.version.

cost_per_point: the benchmarking unit

cost_per_point = total_cost_usd / weighted_points — USD per weighted outcome point. It is the inverse-unit dual of TIER (numerically 1000 / tier when both are defined) and is the unit TIER should be benchmarked and trended on:

  • It reads as money a CFO already understands ("$2.40 per weighted point").
  • Constant-price self-comparison requires matching resolved price fingerprints, the original pricing basis of stored rows (or an explicit common reprice), rubric, release and population. A loaded price_table.version alone does not establish that older rows used the same rates; see technical reference §2.1.
  • It is guarded on the points denominator: a zero-point row reports null (rendered —), never a divide-by-zero and never a 0 that would read as the most efficient row (#472). (A $0-cost row with points reports a legitimate 0.0, already surfaced by the zero-token tripwire #136.)

Self-relative interval (not an absolute rank)

In v0.6.1, #1233 withholds intervals when the row's own attributed-cost share is below 50%; exactly 50% passes. When shown they are labelled "excludes uncertainty from unattributed spend". Team and division rows carry none.

/scores also returns cost_per_point_ci_low/high: the nominal 95% percentile-bootstrap bounds (coverage unvalidated) for a ranked developer's cost_per_point, derived by reciprocal transform from the same TIER bootstrap (#133) — it expresses cost_per_point against that developer's own resampled outcome history, the honest thing a single window supports. It is not a percentile rank against other developers or other orgs. TIER does not publish a cross-org distribution or percentile. Compare a team with its own past.

What you may and may not compare

Comparison Allowed? Unit Condition
Same developer/team over time Conditional cost_per_point Match resolved price fingerprints, historical row pricing or an explicit common reprice, rubric, release, population and capture. Matching loaded version numbers alone is insufficient.
Same developer/team, within one work_type, over time Conditional cost_per_point (per segment) As above; compare a segment only to the same segment.
Two organisations No cross-organisation comparison cost_per_point Matching stamps do not establish shared calibration, capture or task mix. Within one organisation, a comparison remains a question to investigate under the technical reference §2.1 conditions.
Two parties, mismatched rubric.version or price_table.version No — Non-comparable by construction. This is the guard, not a limitation.
Raw tier across parties No cross-organisation comparison — Neither tier nor cost_per_point makes differently calibrated organisations comparable; matched stamps do not establish equivalence.
Cross-type (feature vs security) No — A category error (#187). Compare within a work_type only.
Against an absolute "good" value No — No such value exists. TIER has no reference cohort baked in, by design.

Rubric 2: corrective work (operator opt-in)

The size scale and work-type taxonomy above are unchanged. The default-off outcomes.event3_scoring switch selects the correction rule and rubric together from persisted state. One transaction resolves every outcome's complete chain and pins rubric 2; unresolved enabled state refuses scores and new seals. Disabling restores legacy scoring and rubric 1. Lifecycle confirmation alone never enables scoring.

Rubric 2 retains the quality floors above and adds this step when an enabled correction occurs within the inclusive 14-day window after its parent merged:

Parent's own quality before correction Corrected quality
Clean: 1 17/20 (0.85)
CI failure: 0.7 11/20 (0.55)
Other quality values Unchanged

This step applies once to the parent's own quality, including when the parent is itself a follow-up. An unconfirmed cross-author declaration does not enable it.

Under rubric 2, an original receives max(0.1 * W_original, q_original_corrected * W_original + sum(credit) - sum(revised_weight)). Here q_original_corrected is the original's own quality after the correction step above; it never reads a follow-up's quality. A follow-up receives its allocated share multiplied by its own quality, never its parent's quality. Repairing a repair runs the allocation on the parent's share and replaces it. Stored rational inputs use exact math/big.Rat values in lowest terms. Raw activity (outcome_n) remains separate from eligible growth (sample_n); repair-only activity cannot inflate the ranking evidence floor. Same-author declarations are provisional until operator review. Cross-author credit requires confirmation; multi-root foreign relations remain pending. An own-root multi-root declaration takes the least-favourable own-root result. Disputes and withdrawals after confirmation retain credit until operator settlement. Team/division diagnostics are not_shown.

Rubrics 1 and 2 each verify under their own rule; this change does not move the weight scale. Unknown rubric versions remain a changed-definition signal. Sealed comparisons reject differing rubric versions, and existing seals retain their original bytes and digest. A shared rubric still does not establish comparable calibration, capture completeness, or task mix.

See Enable Event 3 scoring for configuration, rollback and startup behavior.

Reviewed allocation and review limits

Reviewed cumulative scope before and after the correction determines marginal growth; an explicit pure repair has zero marginal growth. This differs from the follow-up's reviewed own size and its individual allocated share. The original is charged first, retaining its floor; the remaining budget goes to follow-ups in canonical order, capped at their reviewed own sizes. Their shares then take their own quality. For a clean original of weight 5, corrected quality 0.85 and a clean pure repair of size 1, the original receives 3.25 points and the fixer receives 1: total 4.25, with zero added growth and no repair-only SampleN credit.

Growth is read on the same 0.5–8 scale, so it stops at the top bucket. When the chain's cumulative scope before a follow-up already sizes at 8, whether reviewed or bucketed from churn, the follow-up adds zero growth however much new scope it delivers. For a confirmed or same-author pair, its share is then charged against the original; either way it adds no SampleN credit. New scope merged as its own PR that declares no parent (Corrects: none) is sized and credited on its own.

Unconfirmed eligible same-author pairs use recorded churn and file counts as a provisional approximation, not reviewed delivered growth. Missing sizing can remain pending; unconfirmed foreign roots cannot move another author's points. Reviewed per-root shares for multiple originals are planned, not shipped. Actor/role are self-asserted local CLI input, not authentication; single-operator installations confirm their own pairs. The required distinct actor label is advisory and does not prove independent review. Disputed status appears in developer/operator views, exports, the corrective digest and new seals, while team/division views return not_shown. Disputes and withdrawals after confirmation retain prior treatment until operator settlement. Existing sealed reports never change.

See Event 3 coverage for recorded declarations and limits.
